Quasar 4C 04.81

I use absorption lines to study the gas in front of the quasar and ask what they can tell us about the emitting region behind it.

What the spectra measure

Gas at redshift z ≈ 2.58 absorbs light from 4C 04.81. I analyze VLT/UVES and X-Shooter spectra to study partial covering. If the gas covers only part of the emitting region, even a strongly absorbed line can retain flux from the uncovered part.

I extended SPECTRO to fit homogeneous and inhomogeneous partial-covering models. I also developed a bottom-flux module and analyzed 36 singly ionized iron (Fe II) transitions from six lower levels. The residual flux at the line minima constrains effective covering, while joint fits to the full profiles use information across pixels.

Fe II covering and joint fits

The nominal excitation-dependent covering trend was 4.3σ. It weakened to about 2σ after I excluded two influential transitions overlapping broad quasar emission. The strength of the trend therefore depends on which lines enter the fit.

A separate joint fit of Fe II and excited Fe II over 830 spectral pixels favored separate inhomogeneous absorber screens with an uncovered fraction over a common screen (ΔBIC ≈ 122). This is a preference between the tested model families. Several physical arrangements could produce similar absorption profiles.

Excitation, density and wavelength

The Fe II level populations describe the excitation of the gas. I use statistical-equilibrium calculations with CHIANTI atomic data to explore how the populations depend on physical conditions such as electron density. Density inference depends on the excitation model. Effective covering also depends on how the gas is distributed across the emitting region.

To look for wavelength-dependent covering, I compare transitions from the same lower level and check the sensitivity to line selection and instrumental broadening. The Fe II data leave a common wavelength slope uncertain.

Excitation and instrumental checks

Disk temperature and absorber geometry

I model how different absorbing screens would cover an accretion disk whose brightness changes with wavelength. An offset screen and a change in the disk’s radial temperature profile can both change the covered flux. Under the tested opacity model, the absorption left the disk temperature exponent β poorly constrained over 0.2–1.5.

Ongoing work

I’m developing a joint fit for the excitation and absorption profiles using Markov chain Monte Carlo. The aim is to see which constraints survive when level populations, covering and line-profile uncertainties are inferred together.
